Police & Arrest Rights in Nigeria

What the police can and cannot do, what to say if you are questioned or arrested, bail rules, detention limits, and how to report extortion.

The 30-second version:
  1. You have the right to remain silent. Use it.
  2. Ask: "Am I under arrest? What is my offence?" Officers must inform you.
  3. You have the right to call a lawyer and inform family members immediately.
  4. You must be charged to court within 24 to 48 hours, or released on bail.
  5. Bail is free. Never pay money for bail.
  6. Stay calm and do not resist physically. Note badge names and report afterwards.

1. Your 6 core rights when arrested

These protections are established under Section 35 (personal liberty) and Section 36 (fair hearing) of the 1999 Constitution, plus the Police Act 2020:

  1. Right to be told why (S.35(3)): Police must promptly explain the reason for your arrest in words you understand.
  2. Right to remain silent (S.35(2), ACJA S.6): You cannot be forced to write or sign a statement. Anything you say can be used in court.
  3. Right to a lawyer of your choice: If you cannot afford a private lawyer, the Legal Aid Council provides free criminal defense.
  4. Right to be charged quickly (S.35(4)-(5)): Police must bring you before a court within 24 hours (if a court is within 40km) or 48 hours (if no court is nearby), or release you on bail.
  5. Right to dignity (S.34, Anti-Torture Act 2017): Beating, torture, threats, or forcing confessions is strictly illegal under Nigerian law.
  6. Right to bail: Under the law, suspects are presumed innocent and entitled to bail for non-capital offences.

2. What police CAN and CANNOT do

Police CAN

  • Stop and ask questions if they reasonably suspect an offence (Police Act 2020, S.48-50).
  • Search your person or vehicle if they have reasonable suspicion or a valid search warrant.
  • Arrest a suspect if they have reasonable grounds to believe an offence was committed.
  • Take your statement, but only voluntarily after cautioning you of your right to remain silent.
  • Detain a suspect briefly for investigation within the constitutional 24/48-hour limit.

Police CANNOT

  • Arrest you for a civil matter such as rent dispute, unpaid personal loan, or breach of contract (strictly barred by Police Act 2020, S.32).
  • Arrest you in place of someone else (proxy arrest of mother, spouse, or sibling is unlawful).
  • Demand money for bail, fuel, statement papers, or administrative fees.
  • Search your phone or laptop without your voluntary consent or a valid court warrant.
  • Subject you to physical assault, torture, or public parade before trial.
  • Detain you past 48 hours without a judicial remand order from a magistrate.

3. What to do if stopped or searched

  1. Stay calm and keep your hands visible. Do not run, argue aggressively, or reach into bags suddenly.
  2. Ask politely: "Good afternoon officer, please what is the reason for this stop?" Note their name tag, uniform service number, vehicle plate, time, and location.
  3. Filming in public: There is no Nigerian law forbidding citizens from recording public encounters. If officers object, remain calm and keep audio recording safely.
  4. Phone searches: You can state: "Officer, I do not consent to a search of my personal phone without a court warrant." If they force you, do not resist physically; note their names and file a formal report.
  5. Never sign what you have not read. Carefully read every line before signing. You have the right to write: "I decline to make a statement until I consult my lawyer."
What to say (English):
"Officer, am I under arrest or am I free to go? Please tell me the offence. I wish to remain silent and contact my family and lawyer."

Wetin to talk (Pidgin):
"Officer, abeg I wan know: una don arrest me abi I fit go? Abeg tell me wetin be my offence. I no wan write statement now, I wan call my lawyer and family."

4. At the police station & detention limits

  • Cautionary statement: Before asking you to write, officers must caution you. You can choose not to write. If you do write, keep facts clear and sign only your words.
  • Detention limits: Section 35(4)-(5) of the Constitution requires police to charge you to court or grant bail within 24 hours (or 48 hours if no court is nearby). Longer detention requires an order from a judge.
  • Access to phone & family: You are legally entitled to contact family and speak with a legal practitioner. Denial of phone calls is an actionable violation.
  • Detainee welfare: Suspects are entitled to basic medical care and humane treatment under the ACJA. Indefinite holding for "investigation" is unconstitutional.
Warning: Never pay money for bail. Never sign a blank sheet. Never accept to pay a complainant money at the station to secure your release if you did not commit an offence. Demand to see a lawyer.

5. Bail rules & fees

  • Police bail (administrative bail): For bailable offences, bail is granted at the police station with a reliable surety (a person who guarantees your court appearance). No fee is required.
  • What a surety provides: Valid identification, proof of address, and passport photographs. A surety does not pay cash to the police.
  • Court bail: If charged to court, your defense counsel applies for bail before the magistrate or judge.
  • If an officer demands ₦20,000 or ₦50,000 for bail: that is criminal extortion. Politely refuse, record names, and file an official complaint.

6. How to report police misconduct

  1. Preserve all evidence: Officer names, badge numbers, patrol vehicle registration, station division, date, time, and witness contacts. If assaulted, obtain a medical report from a government hospital immediately.
  2. Submit complaints directly to:
    • Police Complaint Response Unit (CRU): Official complaints desk dedicated to investigating rogue officers.
    • Police Service Commission (PSC): Statutory supervisory oversight body for the Nigeria Police Force.
    • National Human Rights Commission (NHRC): Handles extrajudicial actions and severe human rights violations.
    • Legal Aid Council of Nigeria: Provides free legal assistance for indigent citizens facing prosecution.
  3. Document your timeline: Keep a clear 1-page summary: what happened, when, where, who was involved, and evidence collected.
  4. Enforce rights in court: You can file a Fundamental Rights Enforcement suit at the State or Federal High Court for unlawful detention and extortion.
